# 5/3/2026 1 AM - Routed Charging Dock PCB

_Time spent: 6h_

I started to route the PCB for the charging dock. When I first imported the footprints for each of the components, they seemed to be too large to fit on the tiny 15x30(ish) millimeter PCB, but that's when I started to employ some crazy Tetris skills to fit everything in. I started with placing the components for the battery charger circuits as efficiently as possible, and I ended up with a very small number of total vias upon routing the tracks.

![Screenshot 2026-05-03 at 11.22.06 am](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1777771331961-datl29.png)

Still, I figured that I would need to take a multi-part board approach in this design by creating two separate boards and stacking them together (just like how the studs themselves are made). The LEDs and the USB-C port took up the most amout of space, so I initially placed them on a separate board to the main charger block.

Placing the MAX17048X+T10 chips and routing their signals, I realised that maybe I would be able to fit almost everything on one board, especially since I had more than enough space left in the middle for a USB-C port and the ATMega328P to comfortably fit in. This is what I ended up doing, with the LEDs and a mysterious switch for the ATMega328P still being located on a separate board. In the remaining spaces left on the main part of the board, I squeezed in test points that will be used for programming and monitoring the signals that go between the MCU and its various peripherals.

![Screenshot 2026-05-03 at 11.22.54 am](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1777771383648-zcx673.png)

![Screenshot 2026-05-03 at 11.22.33 am](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1777771370080-w3lt6u.png)

I employed the same stacked board design as I did in the studs themselves, with some test points between the two boards that will be connected together with a low-temp solder after assembly (to make sure any other pre-soldered parts don't fall off). For the connection to GND, I decided not to use a test point but instead used the through-holes required for the shield in the USB-C port on the main board. The through-hole pins extend a bit outwards, so accompanying holes on the PCB fit into them which also provides robust alignment for everything.

Routing everything was quite difficult especially given JLCPCB's minimum via size was quite large compared to the tiny board I had. Nevertheless, I was able to fit in a largely uninterrupted ground plane and robust traces for power to everything as well as multiple test pads for debugging and programming. Considering I use the same 0.4mm minimum 4-layer PCB thickness, the whole board exclusing components will be 0.8mm thick, just enough to fit into the enclosure without serious modifications to it.

I chose to panelise the two parts of the PCB for cheaper assembly costs (just like the studs), and connected them together using a mouse bite footprint.

Now it was time to troubleshoot. I ran the Design Rules Checker and found hundreds of errors. This was, however, mostly due to clearance issues and me forgetting to reduce the allowable pad clearance (as JLCPCB can support it). After edding those rules and clearing up some minor issues, the PCB was fully done!

# 4/20/2026 7 AM - Created Charging Dock Schematic

With the basic outline and features I wanted to include in the charging dock finalised, I started to research the parts which I would include, the first one being the battery charging ICs.

These will be the cornerstone of the dock board, and they need to charge fast without an overly complicated external circuit. After a lot of research (which included charging ICs from Analog Devices and various others from Texas Instruments), I settled on using the BQ24075 from Texas Instruments for a numbr of reasons. First, it has a standalone mode which does not require linking it up to a microcontroller via I2C (which would require extra firmware development for drivers), and it achieves very fast charging speeds at 800mA, which can ideally charge the 40mAh batteries of the earrings in around 3 minutes! # 3/24/2026 3 AM - Designed PCB schematic and started PCB design

_Time spent: 3.25h_

After deciding on the basic SMD components for the MatrixStuds, I started to compile together a simple schematic. As the microcontroller, the IMU and the voltage regulator all require a very small number of external components, implementing the necessary application schematics for their operation was quite straightforward.

![Screenshot 2026-03-24 at 2.06.32 pm](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1774321612546-9hm52s.png)

After that, I created the schematic for the simple 4x4 LED matrix, which uses a total of 8 GPIO pins on the microcontroller. The pins for this matrix are not actually connected to the microcontroller in the schematic, but are connected to touch points that correspond wigth equivalent contacts that connect to the microcontroller. This is my solution to two significant problems:
1. There are not enough layers in a 4-layer board to support the routing and vias for both the control electronics and LED matrix
2. A 2-sided board is pretty much mandatory to minimise space taken up, but that incurs additional assembly costs

Having the control electronics (MCU, accelerometer, voltage regulator) on a separate one-sided board, while having the LEDs on another one-sided board would solve these problems, as when these two boards are stacked up, it effectively makes an 8-layer 2-sided PCB for the price of a 4-layer one-sided PCB.

The touch points are on the backside of each board, and allow the two boards to interface with each other.

A couple more touch points have also been added to the front side of the electronics board to allow for programming and the battery connection.

With the super simple schematic done, it was time to move onto designing the PCB, which, as it turns out, is a completely different beast.

That doesn't really worry me as I start laying out the matrix of LEDs on a really nice 8x8mm square board outline with rounded edges. Routing all the signals is trivial, and there is enough space between each LED to  allow for relatively large vias that are nowhere close to the minimum size JLCPCB can manufacture without a price jump. To make the board look cooler (because let's face it, the whole point of matrix LED studs is aesthetics) the LEDs have been tilted at a 45° angle, which also comes with the benefit of easier routing and a denser matrix.

# 3/22/2026 3 AM - Initial Design and Component Selection

_Time spent: 3.5h_

One day, I found myself very intrigued by this video from YouTube creator Mitxela (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CHoGIvOi-jw) and decided that making some cool matrix earrings would be a fun way to master my skills in the miniaturisation of electronics.

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1774149397835-czvpk0.png)
*image credit: mitxela*

However, I wanted my design to be completely custom, and that includes the enclosure. More importantly, I also wanted my design to be as small as possible (less than 10mmx10mm) while still integrating a microcontroller and *some* sensor so that the studs would react to changes in their environment. 

Since these earrings would be so small, I was also prepared to make sacrifices on the actual LED matrix, as having an especially high-resolution matrix in an area less than 1cm^2 would be a waste of power and introduce significant complexity (Also, having an especially dense board would require a lot of vias, which take up additional space as JLCPCB can't manufacture boards with blind or buried vias.

The third unique thing I wanted to have in my design was a rechargeable Li-ion battery, the same as the ones used in wireless earbuds. This also means that I'll have to create a battery charging circuit for the MatrixStuds, probably in a sort of case.

So, with my ideation complete, I set out on a hunt for components that would fit my extremely constrained dimensional requirements. Researching on various forums and articles, I found some strong contenders for what microcontroller I would include. Since this microcontroller would have to have enough memory to load in various animations, and enough computing power to run some sort of algorithm to control the matrix using sensor input, have enough GPIO pins to drive a reasonabler matrix, and consume a small enough emount of power to last at least a whole day from a tiny 25mAh battery, this narrowed the search down significantly. At the end, I settled on the MAX32660 microcontroller, which is quite computationally beefy for its tiny 3x3mm size desppite being one of the most efficient MCUs I could find.

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1774149292266-i2mojm.png)

After that, I started looking for some sensor to put in the earrings so that it would actually feel "alive". Initially, I thought about using a light sensor, or a very small microphone to respond to voice, but in the end I chose to go with a ridculously tiny 3-axis accelerometer by Bosch: the BMA530. This sensor is not far from the size of an 0201 resistor, measuring only 1.2mm on its longest axis. This would free up a ton of space on the board, and since it uses Bosch's very easy-to-use Sensor API, it can interface with the microcontroller effortlesly.

![image](https://stasis.hackclub-assets.com/images/1774149333419-e5vnmi.png)

As the MAX32660 cannot handle any supply voltages above 3.6V (As it is probably designed to be powered by 3V coin cells), I would have to take the 3.7V nominal voltage from a Li-Po battery and step it down to 3.3V for the components. For this, I chose the XCL202B331ER-G by TOREX for its 2.5x2mm footprint. As for the rest of the components, I will be using 0201 passives where possible, and the LEDs will be 0201-sized in a cool white colour. 

As the MAX32660 package I selected has around 9 usable GPIOs, I will connect the chip to a 4x4 matrix. Yes, I did consider Charlieplexing for way more LEDs, but that would've been a pain to arrange and code for, and would've also increased ther size of the studs.

Since the component list for this thing is so tiny, I can jump straight into modeling the PCB!
